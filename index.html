Section 01 · The unit

No buttons. No screen. No lights.

The shape carries the style: cut edges, clipped corners, sides that angle in toward the wall, a gap where the cover meets the base, and the EMBER wordmark. Every picture here is path-traced from the CAD model with Mitsuba 3, and lit like the room the unit is for: a lamp on the left and low sun from the right.

Section 02 · What it does

Turns the dial you already have.

Ember fits on a line-voltage dial thermostat that switches electric in-floor heating. It has one job: turn that dial to off, or to on, when the Raspberry Pi tells it to.

  • F-01

    Uses the thermostat on the wall

    Ember replaces only the snap-on plastic cover of an 859M dial thermostat. The thermostat's metal plate and its two screws stay where they are.

  • F-02

    On and off from the Raspberry Pi

    The Pi sends the command over Bluetooth Low Energy. A Seeed XIAO nRF52840 receives it and turns the dial with a 28BYJ-48 stepper motor.

  • F-03

    Knows where the dial is

    An AS5600 magnetic sensor on the dial shaft reads its angle in steps of 0.088 degrees. If the angle does not change while the motor runs, the motor stops and the unit reports it.

  • F-04

    Three AA cells

    About 2.4 years on alkaline cells when the heating switches 4 times a day from October to April (calculated). The cells change without taking the unit off the wall.

  • F-05

    Comes off in one piece

    Loosen the two side screws and pull the unit straight off: case, motor, cord drive and sensor come away together. The sector is a push fit on the dial shaft, so the original knob goes back on.

  • F-06

    Keeps clear of the mains parts

    The plate has openings that show the thermostat's mains-voltage parts. No screw, contact, magnet or wire goes near them, and solid plastic closes them off. The USB port faces the bottom wall, out of sight.

Section 04 · Cord drive

6.30 turns of the motor for one turn of the dial.

The motor turns a small grooved capstan. A braided cord 0.50 mm across is wrapped round the capstan, and its two ends are held on a sector (a slice of a disc) fixed to the dial shaft. The cord sits at a radius of 5.66 mm on the capstan and 35.66 mm on the sector, so the dial turns 6.30 times slower than the motor and gets 6.30 times its torque, less friction.

Drawn to scale from the v6 numbers, shapes simplified. Shaft spacing 42 mm. Scale: degrees of travel from OFF.

The buttons play a move 10 times faster than the motor makes it. A full move is 12,902 half steps at 2.0 ms each: 25.8 s. The thermostat switches on 140 degrees past its LO stop, which is 120 degrees of travel from OFF. OFF is 20 degrees short of the LO stop, so the drive never presses the dial against it.

Section 05 · Power

Three AA cells, no charger, no regulator.

The cells feed the XIAO's battery pads and the motor driver directly. Between moves the motor coils and the angle sensor are switched off; only the radio stays on, so the Pi can connect.

Section 06 · Circuit

Fifteen wires, thirteen solder joints.

A Seeed XIAO nRF52840 drives the motor through a ULN2003 board and reads the AS5600 sensor over I2C. The sensor is powered from two XIAO pins, only while it reads. The motor's current returns straight to the cells, not across the XIAO's board.

Spare D2 · D3 · D6

Take one cell out before connecting USB

The battery pads also feed the XIAO's lithium charger. With USB connected it would try to charge the AA cells, which can make them leak.

  • Three fresh alkaline cells give about 4.8 V. Every part on the XIAO's battery pads is rated for at least 6 V, so there is about 1 V to spare. Four cells (about 6.4 V) would be over that limit.
  • The step signals use D7 to D10. The sensor wires leave along the XIAO's USB-C end and the motor wires from the top of the other row, so the two groups do not cross.
  • Wires with a socket at one end are female-to-female jumpers cut in half, so no other hookup wire is needed.

Section 07 · Design history

Thirteen versions in one day.

On 2026-10-15 each version of the case was built by its own script, saved with its own STEP file, opened in Autodesk Fusion and reviewed. The verdict on each card is the one given at the time. The lamp is lit on each version that was liked or carried forward.

  1. v1

    One box: slanted front, three colour stripes, a grille and a ring panel over the dial.

    Too much like the 1980s, not enough futurism.

  2. v2

    Separate raised forms: a battery pod and an eight-sided dial pod.

    Not reviewed; reference images came instead.

  3. v3

    A flat cover in a deeper base, a large plain disc over the dial, round vent holes.

    Not interesting enough: wanted interesting edges and corners, and sides that angle in.

  4. v4

    The sides angle in toward the wall; the front edges are cut by different amounts; an L-shaped battery cover.

    Liked. Saved as a favourite.

  5. v5 seen from below

    No disc. Top and bottom angle in steeply, leaving the plate's edges open to the room.

    Interesting, but the angle is too steep and goes too far in.

  6. v6 seen from below

    Flat top and bottom, with a port over each of the plate's edges.

    Loved. Saved as a favourite.

  7. v7 seen from below

    Three tries at a new direction: asymmetric cuts, a tower and pod, then a hull with a fin and a drum over the dial.

    Still too close to the earlier designs.

  8. v6.1 – v6.2 seen from below

    Back to v6: EMBER on the front, finger indents, an open gap where cover meets base. Then the Stop letters in a frame.

    The design to go with.

  9. v6.3 – v6.5

    Fitted to the thermostat as measured with calipers; side screws; the same 16 mm clip on all four corners.

    Looks good; wanted the screws on opposite sides.

  10. v6.6 working version

    One side screw each side, directly opposite; a lip and a 3.0 mm gap all round. Changed in place from here on.

    Right, and moving in the direction wanted.
